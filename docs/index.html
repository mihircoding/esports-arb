Esports Arb

Four studies of the same market: cross-venue arbitrage, market making, how fast mispricings disappear, and whether the match, map, handicap and totals markets agree with each other. Every result comes from real order books, the public trade tape and actual settlements.

Method

What is being priced

A head-to-head esports match has two outcomes, so a contract paying $1 if team A wins and a contract paying $1 if team B wins together pay exactly $1. Everything here follows from that, plus each venue's fee schedule.

Both exchange fees scale with the variance of the payoff, so they peak at 50c and shrink at the extremes. A pair of 50c legs needs about 3c of mispricing to clear fees.

Matching the same match across venues

  • Team names are folded (generic tokens dropped, alias table, token-subset rule) and scored by similarity.
  • "Spirit" and "Spirit Academy" score low on purpose: a false match would create a fake arbitrage.
  • Start times must agree within six hours; Kalshi encodes its start in the event ticker, in US Eastern.
  • Sizing walks both order books and stops when the marginal pair stops paying after fees.

Result — market making

Quoting works better than taking, but only away from the start

Kalshi charges no maker fee on esports, so resting orders that fill cost nothing. The maker quotes around a fair value that blends Kalshi's and Polymarket's mids, skews against inventory, and never trades in play. Settings were chosen on 588 matches and tested once on the next 589, replayed against Kalshi's real trade tape.

The spread earned is the same all day; what changes is who is trading. Inside two hours of the start, the flow knows something — lineups, stand-ins, late news — and takes roughly twice the spread back. Not quoting after T−120 minutes was the single largest improvement in the study.

At 60-second latency the strategy makes 2.8c per contract. At 10 minutes it makes nothing, so the live loop runs on websockets.
Result — how long mispricings last

Most are gone inside a second

Live order books over websockets, re-checking only the match whose book changed — about 33 microseconds per update, keeping up with 300–440 Polymarket messages a second. Each arbitrage is logged as an episode with a millisecond lifetime.

Pre-match episodes are rarer, a few contracts deep, and some last minutes. Polymarket moves first in nearly every case, which is why it anchors the maker's fair value.

Result — series structure

The map, handicap and totals markets do not agree with the match market

A best-of-three ends in one of six map sequences, so every match, map, handicap and totals contract is a payoff vector over those six. A linear program searches both venues for the cheapest basket that pays at least $1 in every sequence; under $1 after fees is an arbitrage rather than a forecast.

The Dota 2 basket above priced "Dawn Bulls 2-0" at about 50% while pricing "Dawn Bulls win at all" at about the same, which implies a 2-1 win cannot happen. Betting the handicap mispricing returned +22.6c per bet out of sample at midpoint prices, but only 8 of 107 signals had a real trade on the needed side before the match: those books are mostly empty. That points at resting limit orders rather than taking.

Engineering

What is in the repo

Modules

  • connectors/ — Kalshi, Polymarket, sportsbook odds; ask ladders rebuilt from the opposite side's bids.
  • arb.py — fee-aware edge, depth walk, exact fee rounding, cancelled-match P&L.
  • mm/ — quoting, inventory, event-driven backtest, paper and live loops with exposure caps and a kill switch.
  • streaming/ — L2 books from snapshots and deltas, sequence-gap resync, episode logging.
  • series/ — outcome states, the arbitrage LP, settled-match study.

Testing

  • 79 tests: fee rounding, depth walking, roster-aware name matching, book maintenance from deltas, request signing, LP identities.
  • Streamed books were checked against the venue's own snapshots: 94% matched exactly, and every mismatch was a size at one level right after a trade.
  • Every backtest chooses its settings on an earlier slice of matches and reports the later slice once.
Limitations

Limitations

  • Sizes are small. The arbitrages that clear fees are typically one contract deep, and the market maker filled 2,501 contracts in ten days.
  • The market-making edge is +2.8c per contract with t = 1.12. The true edge could be zero.
  • Kalshi's websocket needs an API key, so the streaming study polled Kalshi once a second; in-play episode lifetimes are bounded by that.
  • Polymarket price history is midpoints, not asks, which is why the series study re-checks every signal against real taker prints.
  • A cancelled match settles differently on each venue — 50-50 on Polymarket, "fair value" on Kalshi, void at a sportsbook — so a hedge can pay less than $1. Unknown settlements are counted as zero.
  • Polymarket's international exchange blocks US users. This is research code, not financial advice.
